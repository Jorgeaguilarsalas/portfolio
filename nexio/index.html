Context

The regulatory gap beneath Berlin's fleet operators.

Fleet operators running PHV vehicles under Germany's PBefG framework and Berlin's LABO reporting requirements face an operational contradiction: the regulation demands audit-ready records of driver activity, working hours, breaks, mileage, and shift integrity — but the tools available to operators are still spreadsheets, WhatsApp threads, and end-of-week reconstructions.

The result is a compliance workflow that is reactive rather than continuous. Reports are assembled from memory. Working-time violations surface after the fact. Audits become emergencies. And every operator we spoke with had built their own workaround — each slightly different, each fragile.

Nexio Fleet's thesis was that this gap is not a UX problem or a reporting problem. It is a product problem: the operator needs a system that captures compliance data as a byproduct of running the fleet, not as a separate reporting exercise. My role was to translate that thesis into a product operators would actually use.

Discovery

How regulation became specs.

The starting material was abstract: paragraphs of PBefG and LABO text describing what regulators expect operators to prove. The finishing material had to be concrete: fields in a database, screens in an app, buttons a driver would tap in the middle of a shift.

The bridge between the two was field discovery. I ran structured conversations with fleet operators and drivers to understand how compliance actually gets done today — not how the regulation reads on paper, but how the operator survives the audit.

The shifts & breaks moment.

Our initial spec for the working-hours module was straightforward: track when a shift starts and ends, log the total. Compliance-adjacent, not compliance-native.

Piloting with operators surfaced the real problem. The operator does not care about start and end times in isolation — they care about what happened inside the shift. Where the breaks fell. Whether the driver actually took them. Whether the pattern of the shift matched what regulation requires or drifted into violation territory.

The spec changed. We integrated a break-management and summaries layer into the shifts module — giving operators an overview of driver break patterns and shift integrity rather than a raw log. And we added configurability inside the shift itself, so operators could handle real-world operational exceptions without compromising the audit trail or data integrity.

The lesson was one about product philosophy more than about a specific feature. When a regulated operation meets a rigid schema, the operator will either abandon the tool or corrupt the data around it. The product has to reflect operational reality — with traceability — not enforce an idealised model that reality does not fit.

Prioritisation

What we said no to, and why.

A pre-revenue product with a small engineering team lives or dies by what it leaves out. Three decisions shaped the MVP more than anything we built.

01
Scope discipline: compliance first, adjacent modules later.

Early proposals included driver billing, administrative extras, and operational modules that would have made the product broader. I pushed to leave them out of the MVP. They were adjacent to fleet management, not compliance-critical, and they would not have supported the initial regulatory validation the product had to earn.

The decision was not about saying no; it was about protecting the one thing the product had to be excellent at — audit-ready compliance — before extending into surrounding functionality.

02
Stability before new features.

Once the compliance layer was live with pilot operators, the temptation was to keep shipping. I held the roadmap on shift-synchronisation and data-integrity issues before adding functionality on top.

Compliance data has to be right or it is worse than not having it — a wrong number in an audit trail is a liability, not an asset. Stabilising the foundation before extending was the only defensible sequence.

03
Automation over manual approvals.

Several workflows could have been shipped faster with manual fleet-owner approvals at every step — a familiar early-stage pattern. I designed for automated detection of exceptions and integrity issues instead, even where it meant slower initial shipping.

Manual approvals at every step do not scale past the pilot cohort. Automating the detection layer meant the product could grow with new operators without proportionally growing operator overhead.

Each of these was iterative delivery in practice: ship the minimum functional version that resolves the core problem, postpone enhancements until real usage produces signal. Feature completeness is a trap in a pre-revenue product; product risk management — validating value before adding complexity — is the discipline that keeps a small team alive.

Cross-functional execution

Where product meets operations, sales, and the business model.

In a small early-stage team, the boundary between product and business is porous — and it should be. My work outside the pure product surface shaped what the product ultimately had to be.

I contributed to the design of the tiered compliance pricing alongside the CEO — matching operator size and complexity to differentiated packages, informed directly by what the pilot cohort was willing to pay for and what they saw as table-stakes.

Competitive research mapped adjacent players in fleet management and compliance tooling, sharpening where Nexio's compliance-first positioning was defensible and where it overlapped with generalist tooling.

Fleet partnerships and early GTM positioning came out of the same conversations that drove product discovery. When you are in the field with the pilot cohort every week, the sales pipeline and the product roadmap are the same conversation viewed from two angles.

Feedback loops between operations and product development were what kept these two conversations in sync. Field observations converted into prioritised product improvements every sprint — not as an ad-hoc backchannel, but as a structured cadence.

Reflection

What I would do differently.

Tighter MVP scope from day one.

Rather than planning several modules in parallel, I would centre the first version on a single core flow — ideally compliance reporting end-to-end — and validate it in the field before broadening. The variation between operators was larger than we assumed, and a narrower first cut would have surfaced that variation faster.

Product-management discipline earlier.

Clear prioritisation, sprint planning, and prototype reviews should have been in place from the first week. Establishing the cadence retroactively meant we absorbed rework we could have avoided. In future engagements the operational discipline of product management is not a phase-two problem — it is the day-one foundation.

A structured QA environment before piloting.

Onboarding drivers as testers via TestFlight surfaced a lesson that is easy to underestimate: the success of a pilot depends as much on the distribution and onboarding experience as on the software itself. Activating iOS testers required a chain of steps — Apple ID linkage, TestFlight installation, invitation acceptance, build access — and each step generated friction and support demand from users whose primary job is driving, not device configuration. A dedicated QA environment with a smoother tester onboarding process would have accelerated learning and produced cleaner signal from the pilot.

These lessons now guide how I approach product development: focus, structure, and preparation for scale from the first week.
