Leadership & operations

Leadership & Operations frameworks

How I show up, how I develop people, how I turn goals into execution.

This manual consolidates the methods I have developed to lead service, operations, and teams in technically complex environments. The frameworks are operating tools, not slogans — each one exists to resolve a specific management problem, and each one is meant to be picked up when that problem appears.

They are layered. Leadership principles define how I show up. People management defines how I build performance. Execution management converts goals into delivered outcomes. Operational improvement makes the system itself better. Service and cross-functional delivery is where all of it is applied against a customer outcome.

Layer 01 adapts the work of Leila Hormozi — the operating partner and CEO whose body of work on leadership most closely matches how I think about leading people through ambiguity. Layers 02 to 07 are my own, built from running service delivery and operations teams.

Core philosophy

Clarify the outcome, make ownership explicit, create visibility, diagnose before intervening, and build systems that reduce dependence on constant managerial intervention.

01 — Leadership Principles

How I show up

The behaviour the rest of the system is built on.

Every framework below assumes a leader who is stable under pressure, honest early, consistent in private and in public, clear about what matters, and willing to own outcomes outside their formal scope. Without that, the mechanisms still run — they just stop being trusted.

Marketplace operations produce a constant stream of urgent signals: driver disputes, SLA breaches, cancellation spikes at peak hours, a compliance deadline that moves. Most of it is noise around a much smaller number of real patterns. Emotional regulation is the discipline of telling the two apart before acting — and of not passing the volume of my inbox on to the team.

In practice

  • Separate the decision from the reaction, especially on escalations that arrive angry
  • Read the pattern across a week of tickets before redesigning a process on the strength of one
  • Hold a neutral tone with fleet partners and drivers when the conversation is already hot
  • Absorb pressure coming from above instead of passing it down unfiltered
  • Be the stable point during an incident, when everyone else is improvising
How it shows up in my work

In supply and activation work the loudest problem is rarely the largest one. I keep the operating tempo steady so the team can tell the difference — and so partners and drivers deal with a consistent counterpart rather than whoever I happened to be that day.

Working principle

“The team’s composure is downstream of mine.”

Marketplace operations run on feedback travelling between people who have no authority over each other: Ops and Product, Support and Engineering, and fleet partners who are commercial counterparts rather than employees. Sincere candor means saying the thing that needs saying — to a partner whose performance is sliding, to a product lead whose feature is creating operational friction, to a team member whose quality has dropped — with the evidence attached, and without diluting it into politeness.

In practice

  • Lead with the observation and the number, not with the interpretation
  • Give feedback while it is still one incident, not once it is a pattern and a grievance
  • Tell a fleet partner their numbers are slipping before it becomes a contract conversation
  • Separate a hard message from a harsh one — directness is not a licence
  • Ask the uncomfortable question in the meeting, not in the corridor afterwards
How it shows up in my work

Supply relationships survive honesty far better than they survive silence. Running fleet partner performance at Bliq, the partners who recovered were usually the ones who got the uncomfortable number early, framed against an agreed standard, while there was still time to act on it.

Working principle

“Nobody on my team should have to guess where they stand.”

Regulated fleet work puts you in front of drivers, fleet operators, corporate clients and authorities at the same time — and each of them can verify what you told the others. Unimpeachable character means commitments hold once they turn out to be expensive, and decisions look the same whether or not anyone is checking. In a compliance-first context that is not a values statement; it is the operating requirement.

In practice

  • Keep private conduct matching the public version of it
  • Honour a commitment once it has become inconvenient, or renegotiate it openly and early
  • Own mistakes where they were made, and give credit in the same room
  • Never trade a short-term optic against long-term trust with a partner or a regulator
  • Give every audience the same account of the same situation
How it shows up in my work

Building compliance tooling for LABO and PBefG obligations at Nexio Fleet made the standard concrete: operators were relying on our word about what the system did and did not cover. Being accurate about the limitations was worth considerably more than being impressive about the capabilities.

Working principle

“Trust is built by being predictable, not by appearing perfect.”

Supply strategy, regulatory requirements and cross-functional dependencies each arrive in their own vocabulary, and the ambiguity between them is where operational time disappears. Clarity means compressing that until someone can act without re-deriving the context every time: define the outcome before the method, and write the decision down instead of only saying it in a meeting.

In practice

  • Define the outcome and how it will be measured before agreeing the method
  • Write the decision down — with the owner and the reasoning, not just the task
  • Use language that survives the trip from Ops to Product to a fleet partner
  • Turn a regulatory requirement into a concrete operational rule somebody can follow
  • Cut meeting time by circulating the context in advance
How it shows up in my work

Translating LABO and PBefG requirements into product specs, or an Earnings-per-Hour target into an SLA a fleet partner can actually manage against, is the same exercise: take something that is correct but unusable and make it operable without losing what made it correct.

Working principle

“If the priority is not clear, it is not a priority.”

In marketplace operations the failures that cost most are the ones that fall between functions: an order that fails, a driver who never activates, a regulatory requirement that changed and nobody mapped. Ownership means the useful question is not whose scope this sits in but what has to happen now — and that a handover only counts once the other side has confirmed they have it.

In practice

  • Escalate with a proposed decision, not with an open question
  • Close the loop on problems outside my scope instead of routing them onward
  • Turn “not my job” into “here is who owns it, and I have confirmed they do”
  • Take the blame in public and hand the credit over in public
  • Treat a recurring gap between two teams as something to design away, not to chase weekly
How it shows up in my work

Rebuilding the driver activation funnel at Bolt meant owning steps I did not control — documents, vehicle checks, third-party dependencies — because the activation rate was indifferent to where the organisational boundaries sat.

Working principle

“Ownership is measured by the outcome, not by the boundary of the job description.”

The five leadership principles above are grounded in Leila Hormozi’s leadership framework, adapted here through operational experience in marketplace ecosystems (Bliq, Bolt, Wolt) and compliance-first infrastructure work (Nexio Fleet, Salas UG). The descriptions and applications reflect my own interpretation and practice.

02 — People Management

How I build team performance

Direction, Support, Accountability, Development.

Performance is not a personality trait. It is the product of four conditions a manager controls: whether expectations were clear, whether the person had what they needed, whether commitments were followed up, and whether capability was allowed to grow. When performance drops, one of the four is usually missing.

  • Direction▸
  • Support▸
  • Accountability▸
  • Development▸

Define expectations, priorities, ownership, and how success will be measured — before the work starts, not after it goes wrong.

In practice

  • Set clear goals and expected outcomes
  • Define KPIs or observable success criteria
  • Clarify decision rights and responsibilities
  • Connect individual work to team objectives
Working principle

“You cannot fairly hold someone accountable for an expectation that was never clear.”

Understand what the person needs to succeed and remove the barriers that are not theirs to solve.

In practice

  • Regular 1:1s
  • Understand motivation and individual goals
  • Remove blockers
  • Create psychological safety to surface problems
  • Give autonomy over how outcomes are achieved

Cross-reference: Coaching is the key mechanism of Support — it is how support becomes capability instead of rescue.

Performance requires follow-through, not only clarity and support. Accountability is what makes the first two mean something.

In practice

  • Review commitments consistently
  • Recognize when commitments are met
  • Address gaps early
  • Use evidence rather than assumptions
  • Agree corrective actions and follow up

Cross-reference: Corrective Feedback structures the follow-up conversation so the gap turns into an agreed next step.

Development is not simply “sending people to training.” The goal is progressively increasing the person’s ability to solve problems and make decisions independently.

In practice

  • Identify capability gaps
  • Coach through real problems
  • Give progressively larger ownership
  • Delegate decisions, not only tasks
  • Review decisions and reasoning
  • Create opportunities to practice new capabilities
Working principle

“Can this person own more without requiring constant intervention from me?”

Management practices

The tools inside People Management

Three concrete instruments applied within the system above. Each one resolves a specific problem: how to build judgment, how to run a performance conversation, and how to tell whether the problem is even a people problem.

  • Understand▸
  • Question▸
  • Challenge▸
  • Commit▸
  • Review▸
  • 01UnderstandUnderstand the person’s perspective, context, and reasoning before giving advice.
  • 02QuestionUse questions to help them examine the problem, assumptions, options, and consequences. What do you think is causing this? What options do you see? What would you do if I weren’t available?
  • 03ChallengeTest their reasoning. Surface blind spots and challenge assumptions without taking ownership of the problem away from them.
  • 04CommitConvert the conversation into a decision or a concrete next action that the person owns.
  • 05ReviewReturn to the decision. Evaluate both the outcome and the reasoning: what happened, what did you learn, what would you do differently?
Working principle

“Coaching builds judgment, not dependency.”

  • Observe▸
  • Explain▸
  • Listen▸
  • Agree▸
  • Follow up▸
  • 01ObserveState the factual behavior or performance gap without assigning motive.
  • 02ExplainDescribe the impact and why the issue matters.
  • 03ListenHear the employee’s perspective and diagnose the cause — the natural point to apply Skill / Will / System.
  • 04AgreeDefine the corrective action, the expectation, the support required, the owner, and the timeline.
  • 05Follow upReturn to the commitment. Without this step the conversation was information, not management.
Working principle

“Feedback without an agreed next step is incomplete.”

The same symptom — missed deadlines, quality gaps, escalations — has three very different causes, and each one calls for a different response. Applying the wrong one damages the person and leaves the problem in place.

Skill

Cannot do it yet

The capability is not there. The person is trying and the output still falls short.

Response: training, coaching, methodology, temporary support.

Will

Motivation is limiting

Motivation, confidence, or ownership is the constraint, not capability.

Response: motivation, standards, incentives, accountability.

System

The environment prevents it

A capable, motivated person would still fail in this process.

Response: fix the process, the resource allocation, or the structural issue.

Working principle

“Underperformance is not automatically a people problem. Diagnose before acting.”

03 — Execution Management

How I turn goals into execution

Translate, Assign, Track, Act.

Strategy fails in the gap between an objective and a named person doing something measurable about it on a Tuesday. This layer closes that gap: it converts intent into outcomes, outcomes into ownership, ownership into visible progress, and progress signals into intervention.

  • Translate▸
  • Assign▸
  • Track▸
  • Act▸

Break strategic objectives into measurable operational outcomes.

Ask

What specifically needs to change?

In practice

  • Convert broad objectives into outcomes the operation can actually understand
  • Define a measurable direction — “reduce onboarding time while maintaining activation quality” rather than “improve Service Delivery”

Turn outcomes into explicit commitments.

Ask

Who owns the outcome, and which dependencies exist?

In practice

  • Every meaningful commitment needs an owner
  • Clarify what they control and what they depend on
  • Shared responsibility without a primary owner becomes no responsibility

Create visibility through milestones, KPIs, dashboards, or operating reviews.

Ask

Are we moving toward the outcome?

In practice

  • Dashboards, KPIs, milestones, weekly reviews
  • The purpose is early detection and informed intervention
  • Not micromanagement — tracking exists to enable action, not to report status

Do not simply report the deviation. Intervene.

Ask

What needs to happen now?

In practice

  • Remove blockers, change priorities, reallocate resources
  • Coach, escalate, adjust timelines, or redesign the mechanism
Working principle

“See → Own → Act.”

04 — Operational Improvement

How I improve systems

Diagnose, Prioritize, Build, Own.

Execution management makes the current system deliver. This layer makes the system itself better, so the same problem stops arriving every week. It is the difference between resolving exceptions and removing the conditions that produce them.

  • Diagnose▸
  • Prioritize▸
  • Build▸
  • Own▸

Map the process, listen to the people inside it, collect evidence, establish a baseline, and separate symptoms from root causes before proposing anything.

In practice

  • Listen to customers and frontline employees
  • Map the process or funnel end to end
  • Collect facts and operational data
  • Locate bottlenecks, failure points, and handoff problems
  • Separate symptoms from root causes
  • Establish a measurable baseline before changing the system

A diagnosis usually produces more problems than capacity. The decision lens is Impact × Frequency × Urgency — how much the outcome moves, how often the problem occurs, and how fast it has to be addressed.

Operational KPI buckets

Speed

Process duration

Onboarding time, response time, resolution time.

Reliability

Consistency

SLA attainment, delivery rate, availability.

Quality

Correctness

Error rate, rework, first-time-right.

Operational load

Human effort

Manual interventions, tickets, escalations.

Business lens

Operational movement only matters if it reaches the business. These are the outcomes the four buckets above are supposed to serve.

Retention

How long customers stay

Churn, renewal, contract length.

Customer satisfaction

How the experience is perceived

Complaints, escalations, survey signal.

Cost-to-serve

What each customer costs operationally

Support hours, manual handling, rework.

Unit economics

Contribution margin per unit

Margin per ride, per order, per account.

Scalability

Growing without degrading

Whether volume can double without the system bending.

Example 01
  • Repeated manual exceptions
  • Higher operational load, slower resolution
  • Higher cost-to-serve
  • Pressure on unit economics
Example 02
  • Poor activation process
  • Lower reliability and quality
  • Worse customer experience
  • Potentially lower retention

In operations it is easy to assume that an operational fix will produce better retention or better economics. In reality that connection has to be evidenced, not assumed. We prioritise knowing we are moving an operational indicator that could affect a business outcome — and we say it that way until the data says otherwise.

Working principle

“Observe operational impact first. Investigate business impact carefully — correlation is not causation. The value of the framework is precision about what you can claim.”

Prioritization question

If we fix this, how much does it move the outcome?

Design the mechanism that changes the outcome. A mechanism is rarely just software.

The mechanisms available

  • Processes and SOPs
  • Playbooks and decision rules
  • Ownership and escalation rules
  • Dashboards and operational tooling
  • Automation
  • Meeting and review rhythms
  • SLAs and service standards
  • Training and enablement
  • Customer communication protocols
Objective

Transform an insight into a mechanism that can be executed consistently, by someone other than me.

Implementation is not completion. A process is not successfully implemented until it is monitored, adopted, and owned.

In practice

  • Monitor KPIs and exceptions
  • Review performance at a defined cadence
  • Enforce agreed standards
  • Follow up on commitments
  • Coach where execution is weak
  • Adjust when assumptions prove wrong
Working principle

“Diagnose where it breaks. Prioritize what matters most. Build what fixes it. Own it so it keeps working.”

05 — Service & Cross-Functional Delivery

The disciplines applied

Where the frameworks meet a real customer outcome.

Everything above becomes visible here: a customer who signed a contract has to reach a working service, across teams I do not manage, through systems I do not own, with incidents that do not schedule themselves. These are the six disciplines that make that repeatable.

Service Delivery is the operational discipline of moving a customer from commercial commitment to successfully delivered service. It is considerably broader than ticket handling.

Typical lifecycle

  • Contract signed▸
  • Onboarding▸
  • Order management▸
  • Logistics▸
  • Technical setup▸
  • Activation▸
  • Go-live▸
  • Clean handover▸

Ownership means ensuring the system keeps moving and that nothing important becomes ownerless between two of those stages.

Leadership question

“What needs to happen for this customer to reach a successful go-live, and who owns each dependency?”

  • Promise▸
  • Measure▸
  • Communicate▸
  • Improve▸
  • 01PromiseEstablish a clear baseline: scope, timeline, SLA, responsibilities.
  • 02MeasureCompare actual delivery with the promise — not with a general impression of how it went.
  • 03CommunicateOperational transparency. Customers should not have to chase anyone for status.
  • 04ImprovePerformance data feeds process improvement — this is the handoff back into Operational Improvement.

Service Delivery depends on Engineering, Product, Customer Success, Support, Logistics, and external partners. The leader is accountable for the outcome without hierarchical authority over most of the contributors.

In practice

  • Establish a shared outcome
  • Map dependencies and decision points
  • Clarify ownership across functions
  • Align priorities and deadlines
  • Surface and remove blockers
  • Escalate when local coordination is insufficient
  • Drive decisions and closure
People management

“I manage the person.”

Direction, support, accountability, and development for someone who reports to me.

Cross-functional leadership

“I drive the outcome.”

Accountability for a result delivered by people I do not manage.

  • Understand▸
  • Discuss▸
  • Translate▸
  • 01UnderstandKnow the system functionally: what it does, its components, its dependencies, and how it behaves when it fails.
  • 02DiscussHold substantive conversations with engineers about APIs, integrations, authentication, and dependencies.
  • 03TranslateConvert technical constraints into operational decisions the business can act on.

This is not about writing code. It is about understanding the system well enough that technical reality and operational decisions stay connected.

  • Detect & assess▸
  • Bring the right people▸
  • Map responsibilities & deadlines▸
  • Communicate▸
  • Resolve▸
  • Learn▸
Working principle

“During an incident, uncertainty may be unavoidable. Silence is avoidable.”

Reactive

Customer-reported

Receive → Assess → Coordinate → Communicate → Resolve.

Proactive

Monitoring-led

Monitor → Detect → Act, before the customer notices.

Mature Service Delivery reduces dependence on customers as the monitoring system. Every incident a customer reports first is a gap in detection, not only a gap in the product.

  • Receive▸
  • Update▸
  • Verify▸

Technical services depend on external operators and APIs. When an external party changes a parameter, internal systems must reflect the change — and somebody has to confirm that they did. Skipping the verification step is how silent, delayed failures are created.

06 — Working Principles

What holds across every framework

The rules that do not change when the framework does.

Frameworks are situational; these are not. They are the operating principles that cut across all of the above, and the ones I would keep if every framework on this page were replaced tomorrow.

  1. 01Diagnose before acting.
  2. 02Ownership does not mean doing everything yourself; it means ensuring the outcome has no gaps.
  3. 03Every meaningful commitment needs an owner and visible follow-up.
  4. 04Tracking exists to enable early intervention, not to create reporting for its own sake.
  5. 05Customer communication is part of service performance, not an administrative afterthought.
  6. 06Repeated exceptions are signals that the operating system needs improvement.
  7. 07Underperformance may be Skill, Will, or System; do not assume the person is the root cause.
  8. 08Support and accountability are complementary, not opposing management styles.
  9. 09Cross-functional leadership is the ability to create alignment without relying on hierarchy.
  10. 10Technical fluency is valuable when it enables better operational decisions.
  11. 11A process is not successfully implemented until it is monitored, adopted, and owned.
  12. 12The goal of management is not permanent dependence on the manager; it is stronger capability and autonomy.

07 — How It All Connects

The layered operating model

A system, not a collection of isolated tools.

The frameworks connect in two directions. Vertically, each layer makes the one below it possible: principles make management credible, management makes execution reliable, execution makes improvement measurable, improvement makes delivery repeatable. Situationally, they combine — a single real problem usually pulls in three or four of them at once.

LayerWhat it governsWhat it produces
01 Leadership Principles How I show up → credibility and trust, without which every mechanism below is merely tolerated.
02 People Management How the team performs → capable, accountable owners who can be given real outcomes.
03 Execution Management How goals become work → delivered outcomes with visible progress and early intervention.
04 Operational Improvement How the system evolves → mechanisms that hold so the same failure stops recurring.
05 Service & Cross-Functional Delivery Where it is applied → a customer who reaches a working service, across teams and systems I do not own.

Layer 06 is not a step in this stack. The working principles run through all five, which is why they are stated separately.

Applied management flow

One problem, moving through the system

The layers are easier to judge against a real situation than in the abstract. This is the sequence I actually follow, and which framework is doing the work at each step.

The situation

Go-live dates are slipping. It is not one account — the same delay is appearing across several, the customers are the ones telling us, and the coordinator responsible looks like the common factor.

  1. 1 04 · Diagnose Find where it actually breaks

    Map the delivery funnel end to end and establish a baseline before touching anything. The delay concentrates in technical setup, waiting on a configuration change from an external operator. The coordinator is not the cause; they are the place the delay becomes visible.
  2. 2 02 · Skill / Will / System Separate the person from the system

    Before any performance conversation, name the cause. A capable, motivated coordinator would still miss these dates, because nothing in the process guarantees the external change is confirmed. This is a System problem with a People symptom — and treating it as the reverse would have been both unfair and ineffective.
  3. 3 03 · Translate & Assign Make the outcome and the ownership explicit

    Convert “go-lives are slipping” into a measurable outcome: reduce time from contract to go-live, without degrading activation quality. Then give every dependency in the chain a named owner — including the one that sits with an external partner.
  4. 4 04 · Prioritize & Build Build the mechanism, not the workaround

    Impact × Frequency × Urgency puts the external configuration step first. The mechanism is a set, not a single fix: a Receive → Update → Verify protocol, an escalation rule with a deadline, an SLA agreed with the operator, and a dashboard exception for any setup waiting longer than the agreed window.
  5. 5 05 · Cross-functional leadership Align the people I do not manage

    Engineering, Support, and the external partner all hold part of the chain. Shared outcome, mapped decision points, agreed deadlines, and escalation when local coordination is not enough. No hierarchy involved — the outcome is the authority.
  6. 6 03 · Track & Act Create visibility, then intervene on it

    A weekly operating review on the exception list, not on status. When an account breaches the window, something changes that week: a reallocation, an escalation, a priority swap. Reporting a deviation without acting on it is the failure mode this step exists to prevent.
  7. 7 02 · Development & Coaching Hand the decision over

    Once the mechanism holds, the coordinator owns the escalation decision rather than asking for it. Coach through the real cases, review the reasoning and not only the result, and widen the ownership as the judgment proves out.
  8. 8 04 · Own Keep it working

    Monitor the KPI against the baseline, enforce the standard, and adjust when the assumption turns out to be wrong. Implementation was not the finish line; the mechanism producing the intended result, month after month, is.
